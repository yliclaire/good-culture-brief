Switch to Good Culture when you specifically want a tangier, thicker, more “cultured” cottage cheese and don’t mind paying more. Stay with Daisy if you prefer its milder, classic flavor, distinct curds, lower price, and simple ingredient list. When Good Culture is worth it You want more tang and a richer, creamier body. Good Culture is consistently described as tangier and thicker than Daisy; some reviewers find it almost ricotta-like. You prefer small, tender curds with less watery liquid. Good Culture’s curds are small and tender, while Daisy is known for more distinct, uniform curds. Live cultures are a priority for you. Good Culture emphasizes live and active cultures and specifies strains such as Lactobacillus and Bifidobacterium; Daisy also uses cultures but does not market them as centrally. drteresacrossley.substack You value organic or “clean label” positioning. Good Culture highlights simple ingredients, no artificial ingredients, and family-farm sourcing; it may be the better fit if those attributes matter more than price. You’re using it as a spread or in recipes where thickness helps. Consumer Reports found Good Culture’s dense texture especially good for spreading on toast, though less ideal for eating by the spoonful. When to stick with Daisy Price matters. One 2026 comparison put Good Culture at about 28 cents per ounce versus 17 cents for Daisy—roughly 65% more expensive. You like a milder, less sour dairy flavor. Daisy is generally described as mild and classic; Good Culture is more savory/tangy. You prefer distinct curds over a blended, creamy texture. Daisy preserves individual curds, while Good Culture leans thick and integrated. drteresacrossley.substack You want a proven everyday option. Consumer Reports gave Daisy’s 2% small-curd cottage cheese among its highest overall marks, and found no major problems with the brands tested. Practical recommendation Try Good Culture once if your usual complaint about Daisy is that it is bland, dry, or too curdy. If you already like Daisy’s taste and texture, there’s no compelling nutritional reason to switch—both can be high-protein choices, and sodium varies by product, so compare the label for the specific 2% or 4% version you buy.
