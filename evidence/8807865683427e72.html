Reviewed answer

Honestly, Daisy is a strong default, so switching mostly comes down to preference and priorities rather than one being clearly better. In blind tastings, America’s Test Kitchen named Daisy 4% Milkfat its winner, noting that their top-rated cottage cheeses had fewer ingredients, and tasters preferred drier, bolder, tangier products. Daisy’s very short ingredient list (milk, cream, salt, cultures) is a big part of its appeal. Here’s when Good Culture might make sense for you: You want a different texture. This is the biggest real difference. One tasting panel described Good Culture as having a fluffy, ricotta-like consistency and ranked it best overall, while calling Daisy rich and creamy in both flavor and texture. Shoppers often note Good Culture’s curds are smaller and creamier than other brands. If Daisy ever feels too wet or too uniform to you, try Good Culture. Organic or pasture-raised milk matters to you. Good Culture sells organic lines and emphasizes pasture-raised ingredients. Daisy isn’t organic. onrender You want convenient single-serve cups or flavored varieties. Good Culture leans heavily into grab-and-go cups and fruit-topped options, which can be handy for lunches or snacks. You’re chasing protein per serving. Good Culture markets its organic 2% at 18g of protein per serving, but check serving sizes. Per ounce, the two are usually close, so this is less of a difference than the packaging implies. onrender When to stay with Daisy: if you care most about price (Daisy is typically noticeably cheaper per ounce), use cottage cheese in cooking where texture matters less, or simply like how it tastes. Probiotics aren’t a strong reason to switch either; Daisy also uses live and active cultures. The easiest move is to buy one small Good Culture container alongside your usual Daisy and taste them side by side. Prices and lineups vary by store, so compare the per-ounce price on the shelf tag.
