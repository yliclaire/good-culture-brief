I'm shopping for cottage cheese in the United States. I usually buy Daisy. Under what circumstances, if any, should I switch to Good Culture?

Honestly, Daisy is a hard product to beat. It has only three ingredients, cultured skim milk, cream, and salt, and it won America’s Test Kitchen’s blind cottage cheese tasting. It’s also usually the cheaper of the two: about $3.54 for a 16 oz tub at Walmart (about 22¢/oz). Good Culture tends to cost more, around 29¢/oz for its single-serve cups at H-E-B. So there’s no general reason to switch. It makes sense in a few specific situations. Daisy Pure and Natural Cottage Cheese, 4% Milkfat, 16 oz (1 lb) Tub (Refrigerated) – 13g of Protein per Serving +3 You want organic or pasture-raised milk. This is the clearest reason. Daisy isn’t organic. Good Culture sells organic versions made with pasture-raised milk. onrender You want a named probiotic strain. Daisy’s packaging also says it contains live and active cultures, so this is a smaller difference than the marketing suggests. Good Culture does list a specific strain, Lactobacillus paracasei, on its ingredient label. That matters only if you’re specifically after that strain. You can’t have much lactose. Good Culture has made a lactose-free cottage cheese, and Daisy doesn’t offer one. Check your store, since what’s on the shelf varies. You prefer a thicker, tangier texture, or you mostly buy single cups. Neither brand uses gums. Good Culture is often described as creamy without being runny, with a tangier, yogurt-like flavor. Protein per cup is similar: 19g in a Good Culture 2% 5.3 oz cup versus 17g in a Daisy 4% 5.3 oz cup. Good Culture also sells more flavored and fruit-topped cups, if you like those. I Can Vouch For It! This Cottage Cheese Brand Is Shockingly Good +2 If none of those apply, and especially if you buy the big tubs or cook with cottage cheese, sticking with Daisy is a perfectly good choice. If you’re curious, buying one Good Culture cup to taste next to your Daisy is an easy way to decide.
