Reviewed answer

I would stick with Daisy unless you prefer Good Culture's taste and texture, want certified-organic dairy, or need a lactose-free option. Good Culture is a legitimate alternative, but its nutritional advantages over Daisy are small, and some of its marketing makes the differences seem bigger than they are. The surprising finding is that Daisy already has two of Good Culture's biggest selling points: simple ingredients and live, active cultures. 1. Serving sizes are 113 g and 110 g respectively; labeling and formulations may vary. Good Culture has slightly more protein and fewer carbohydrates, but the differences are nutritionally modest. Both provide substantial protein, and neither contains added sugar in its plain 4% version. If you're trying to increase protein, reduce calories, or improve the overall quality of your diet, switching brands alone is unlikely to make a meaningful difference. 2. When switching actually makes sense You prefer a thicker, denser texture. Good Culture is marketed as thick and creamy, with soft, small curds. Daisy has a more traditional creamy cottage-cheese texture. If you eat it straight from the container, this may matter more than any nutritional difference. Taste is subjective, so it's worth trying Good Culture once. You specifically want certified-organic dairy. Good Culture offers USDA Organic cottage cheese, while regular Daisy is not certified organic. The organic certification provides meaningful production standards, but it does not establish that Good Culture is significantly more nutritious or healthier. You have difficulty digesting lactose. Good Culture sells a dedicated 2% lactose-free cottage cheese. That's a genuinely useful difference if ordinary cottage cheese bothers your stomach. Regular Good Culture still contains lactose. The prices are similar. With only a small nutritional difference, there is little reason to pay a substantial premium for Good Culture Simply. But when the two cost the same, experimenting makes sense. 3. Reasons not to switch Three frequently suggested advantages of Good Culture don't hold up especially well against Daisy. Probiotics: Both brands contain live and active cultures. Good Culture discloses Lactococcus and Lactobacillus groups, but that alone does not establish superior digestive benefits. There isn't convincing head-to-head clinical evidence that Good Culture improves gut health more than Daisy. Cleaner ingredients: Daisy lists cultured skim milk, cream, and salt. Good Culture lists skim milk, whole milk, cream, sea salt, and live active cultures. Neither plain product needs gums, starches, or artificial preservatives. Daisy is already an excellent choice for ingredient simplicity. Higher protein: The roughly 1 gram difference per half-cup serving is too small to matter much for most people. These conclusions are based on the manufacturers' ingredient information, not just their marketing claims. 4. What about price? The price difference depends significantly on which Good Culture version you're considering. Target also lists both regular 4% products at $3.99 per 16-ounce tub, versus $6.59 for Good Culture Organic. That distinction matters: Good Culture Simply is not the same as Good Culture Organic. The organic version is substantially more expensive in these listings, without a corresponding demonstrated nutritional advantage. Both are very good examples of a high-protein, minimally processed dairy food. The one experiment I'd recommend: Buy one 16-ounce tub of regular Good Culture Simply 4% and compare it directly with your usual Daisy 4%. Eat both plain, without fruit or toppings. If you find Good Culture noticeably more enjoyable and the price difference is modest, that's a perfectly good reason to switch. Otherwise, keep buying Daisy. You're not missing out on an important, established health benefit.
