Good Culture has been hard to find for months, so you’re not alone. The brand itself acknowledged on social media that it’s been hard to find, saying demand has been very high and that it’s working to restock without changing its recipe. More broadly, a surge in cottage cheese’s popularity has left shelves empty and stretched manufacturers to their limits, partly because dairies shifted processing capacity toward yogurt decades ago. Good Culture Is Experiencing A Cottage Cheese Shortage & Fans Are Freaking Out +2 My top pick: Daisy. It’s the closest match to what makes Good Culture appealing. The full-fat version has a short ingredient list (cultured milk, cream, salt) with no gums or stabilizers. It’s also sold at most major US grocery chains, so it’s the easiest substitute to actually find. The texture is a bit looser and the curds smaller than Good Culture’s thick, chunky style, but the clean, tangy flavor is similar. If live cultures matter most to you, try Nancy’s. Nancy’s Probiotic Cottage Cheese is organic and made with live, active cultures, which is one of Good Culture’s main selling points. It’s tangier than Good Culture and is easiest to find at natural food stores and Whole Foods. If you value organic or grass-fed milk, try Kalona SuperNatural. It uses minimally processed organic milk and simple ingredients. It’s less widely stocked, but natural grocers often carry it. Whatever you buy, check the ingredient label. Some mainstream brands and store brands add thickeners like guar gum, xanthan gum, or carrageenan. Those aren’t harmful, but they create a smoother, more gelled texture that tastes noticeably different if you’re used to Good Culture. If you mostly care about protein, nearly any 2% or 4% cottage cheese will be similar per serving, so availability and price can decide it.
